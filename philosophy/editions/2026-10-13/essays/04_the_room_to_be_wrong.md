# The Room to Be Wrong

Picture a piece of furniture spread across the floor. There is a diagram, a bag of fittings and one component that you are increasingly certain the manufacturer has supplied incorrectly. Somebody suggests turning it round. You explain that you have already considered that. You have not considered it. You have considered explaining that you have considered it, which is much quicker and preserves your standing as the senior expert on the carpet.

The offending component sits there, unimpressed. It has no access to your educational history. It does not know how competent you are in other settings. You can develop an excellent argument about the quality of the instructions, but the holes remain where they are.

Then the piece is turned. It fits. There is a short silence in which you have to decide what sort of event has just occurred. Has somebody helped you finish the furniture, or attacked the constitutional foundations of your personality?

The difference can occupy the rest of the afternoon.

We laugh because the error is small and the performance is familiar. A person can become so involved in defending an account that the purpose of having the account slips away. The table was supposed to stand up. At some point, a second project began: ensuring that nobody could say you had misunderstood the table. This project has no obvious completion date, and the fittings are becoming cold.

Much of our difficulty with being wrong begins there. An explanation can become attached to a place in a group, a profession, a promise we have made about who we are. Changing it then seems to require more than learning something. It can feel like surrendering a role that has been keeping us recognisable to ourselves and to other people.

A description earns its authority through what it can account for. A person earns dignity through being a person. When those two forms of standing become confused, every correction can sound like an attempt to remove the right to exist. We begin asking an idea to provide the reassurance that should have been available without the idea being correct.

This creates an unfortunate market for certainty. A doubtful answer has to do its work in public. A confident answer can arrive wearing a suit and demand a parking space. We notice fluent speech, a familiar title, the absence of hesitation. These may accompany knowledge, but they cannot perform its verification. An impressive voice can be wrong in precisely the same calm manner in which it was right yesterday.

The appropriate response is to connect confidence to the thing being claimed. Someone may know a subject extremely well. The subject still has a scope. Experience that helps repair a machine does not automatically establish an account of another person's motives. Knowing what usually happens does not remove the need to look at an unusual case. Expertise becomes more useful when its owner can say where the evidence stops.

I have written affectionately about Daddy Pig, whose confidence can survive a navigation error with remarkably little damage to itself. It is a splendid arrangement for a cartoon. In the world beyond the puddle, the consequences have to be included. Getting lost on a picnic and making an unanswerable decision about someone else's life demand different allowances. A willingness to try is valuable. The next step is learning what the attempt has revealed, and who has had to bear its cost.

There is room here for ordinary confidence. You need not reconvene a philosophical congress before using a door handle. Much of what we do depends on reliable learning, familiar conditions and practices we have no reason to reopen every minute. Doubt becomes useful when it has a question to work on. Otherwise it can spend an entire day examining the existence of the kettle while the tea remains unmade.

The important room is the room in which an account can meet evidence that would change it. If every possible result confirms the same belief, the encounter has become ceremonial. Success demonstrates your insight; failure demonstrates the depth of the forces opposing it. Other people's agreement proves that they understand. Their disagreement proves that they do not. It is a very secure arrangement. It is difficult to discover anything while living inside it.

To leave that arrangement, we have to distinguish a feeling about a conclusion from the grounds for it. Being uncertain can feel awful even when the available evidence is genuinely incomplete. Being certain can feel peaceful even when a crucial fact has been missed. Neither feeling needs to be ridiculed. They simply cannot be given the entire job of finding out what is happening.

The same distinction applies to the inherited descriptions we carry. Suppose a child has been called incapable of a task. A successful attempt can be evidence against that description. Giving the child different instruction can also help change what they are able to do. These are related, but they are different events. We may discover that an old judgment was unsupported; we may help create a new capacity. If we confuse them, we can end up celebrating a change in belief while leaving the conditions of learning untouched.

An account of the future should therefore ask what the person is being allowed to attempt. Is the task the same? Is help available? Has there been practice? What outcome would tell us the old summary is too crude? A label such as incapable is cheap to retain because it fits almost anywhere. Understanding a particular difficulty takes more work and offers more places to intervene.

This gives correction a practical form. We identify what a description is supposed to help us read or change, look for a distinction it may be missing, and compare what follows when that distinction is included. The Bounded Observer makes this the starting question of an operational account: which histories can safely share one description for the future actions being considered? A difference that changes those futures has to remain available somehow.

For a person, the question can be immediate. Have you mistaken a refusal for an inability, a delay for indifference, a silence for agreement? Those possibilities do not all deserve equal weight in every situation. They deserve to be distinguished when the evidence leaves them open and the next action depends on which is true. Imagining an alternative becomes useful when it helps us find a way of checking, rather than merely supplying another story to believe.

What happens first can also matter. Imagine a question about a mistake being asked after a public accusation. The person answering may now have to address the accusation as well as the original event. Ask privately, with room to explain, and you have arranged a different encounter. The information you receive may differ because your own action has changed its conditions. Calling yourself an observer does not make the way you ask disappear from the world.

We can see this without reducing a conversation to an exact physical filter. Human encounters include expectations, power, memory and responses that need their own evidence. The practical lesson is to keep the order and conditions of the encounter in the account. If we helped produce the situation we are interpreting, we should be able to say how.

That is especially important when someone has authority. A manager's question and a colleague's question can use the same words while presenting different risks to the person answering. An institution that promises to listen but punishes an unwelcome answer has made silence part of its information system. It may then cite the absence of complaints as evidence that everything is working. The result can be a beautifully self-confirming record of people who have learned what it costs to appear in it.

The room to be wrong is therefore partly a social arrangement. Who can ask for a second look? Who can stop a consequence while a claim is checked? What happens to the person who supplies the inconvenient record? A culture can praise humility and still make correction dangerous. If the powerful are free to revise their explanations while everyone else carries the damage, error has become a privilege rather than a shared opportunity to learn.

There is no need to make every account equally plausible to recognise this. Evidence can support one explanation much more strongly than another. A careful investigation can establish responsibility. The willingness to understand how someone arrived at a belief does not require accepting the belief, forgiving the action or asking a harmed person to remain available for further demonstrations of somebody else's learning.

My ethical commitment begins with the fact that consequences are experienced by people who do not belong to the person making the decision. Their pain, consent, opportunities and ability to challenge a claim matter in their own right. I defend that commitment because a world in which only the decision-maker's experience counts leaves the rest of us exposed to someone else's unchecked account. The framework helps us trace what a decision uses and changes. The value we place on another person's life must be stated and defended as a human commitment.

This is part of what makes responsible choice possible under incomplete understanding. We rarely possess every fact that could matter. We can still ask which action leaves room for correction, which consequence is reversible, what we must check before proceeding and who needs a say. In some circumstances delay is itself costly. Then the task is to act with the best supported account available while keeping its uncertainty visible in the arrangements for what happens next.

It is easier to do that when the material conditions permit it. A person with savings can sometimes wait, obtain advice or leave a job. Someone without them may face a choice between options they reasonably dislike. Calling both people free to choose does not reveal the choices each can actually use. Practical freedom includes the capacity to find an alternative, understand it and act without an intolerable penalty.

Freedom in this sense can grow through ordinary changes to the world. A reliable service creates an option. A safe route makes an action available. Access to a record lets someone challenge a conclusion. A person who can refuse without losing everything has acquired something more substantial than a new attitude towards refusal. The routes themselves have changed.

We do not have to settle every dispute about the ultimate origin of a choice before asking whether these changes matter. Deliberation, learning, seeking advice and organising with others are events within a life. They can contribute to what happens next. Questions about ultimate authorship remain philosophical questions. The practical inquiry concerns which interventions are available, which consequences they produce and how much room people have to take part.

This also makes responsibility more specific. A person has a history. They may have been taught a harmful account or placed in a restricted situation. Those facts can change what a fair judgment of their action requires. We must also examine what they knew, what power they held, what alternatives they could use and how they responded when the consequences became clear. Explanation can help us decide where change is needed. It does not automatically cancel an obligation to repair harm.

The fashionable instruction to change your perspective can become evasive here. A changed interpretation may help someone notice an option, ask a question or attempt something they had ruled out. That action can begin a real change. Yet a belief about a locked door remains different from access to its key. If a problem is being maintained by a condition outside the person, the condition belongs in the work. It cannot be quietly relocated into their attitude because an attitude is cheaper to discuss.

John Snow's work on cholera is useful for that reason. During the Broad Street outbreak, he investigated who had used which water supply, bringing household accounts and the distribution of illness into a different explanation of what was happening. The pump handle was removed. In his own later account, he also recorded that attacks had already declined before that action and that he could not determine whether the well still contained the cause at the time. His argument included evidence and limitations, rather than a tidy story in which one enlightened gesture conclusively ended the outbreak.

The human significance of that investigation includes a change in understanding and an attempt to change exposure. A better account mattered because it directed attention towards a condition that could be acted on. Had people merely felt more hopeful about the same water, the relevant practical question would still have remained. Explanation earns additional value when it identifies something that can make a difference to the outcome.

We should allow the same discipline to reach our own theories. In the earlier hormesis work associated with this programme, claims about the necessity of a beneficial response and a universal peak were too broad. The revised work states exact conclusions for specified response models. Whether a benefit exists depends on the actual relation between the processes being modelled and the endpoint being measured. A finite bound cannot supply a biological benefit that the mechanism does not provide.

The correction matters philosophically as well as scientifically. An appealing pattern can reveal a useful relation, but its beauty does not fill in a missing condition. If the purpose is to understand what the world permits, narrowing a statement to what its grounds support is an improvement. Preserving a larger claim for the sake of its grandeur would leave the next person with a worse tool.

Richard Feynman warned scientists about the ease of fooling themselves and described an integrity that includes making the weaknesses of an account available to others. The useful part of that warning is a practice, rather than a heroic picture of a mind too clever to be deceived. An experiment needs checks that can expose our preferred explanation. A record needs the results we were tempted to leave out. Being aware that people make mistakes is of limited help if we arrange things so that our own mistakes cannot be found.

There is pleasure in understanding something. It is worth protecting that pleasure from the performance of already understanding everything. Curiosity can begin with a confidence sufficient to try and an attention sufficient to notice what the attempt changes. It is allowed to be mischievous. It can interrogate a solemn explanation until the explanation finally admits that it has been built around an assumption nobody checked.

It also needs patience with an answer that is less dramatic than the question. Sometimes the missing fact is small. Sometimes the ordinary comparator works just as well as the elaborate new model. A failed comparison can teach us where a distinction did not help. The world is under no obligation to reward the amount of excitement we brought to the test. It offers an outcome, and we decide whether the outcome is allowed to enter the record.

Return to the furniture. Once the piece fits, the earlier mistake remains an event that happened. It need not become a permanent identity. You can acknowledge it, learn which part of the diagram you missed, and get on with making something usable. If somebody else was inconvenienced, you can make room for that too. A correction that improves the object while requiring the other person to pretend there was never an error is still carrying unfinished work.

Perhaps you pass them the drawing. They can point to the piece; you can look at it together. The agreement now has something beyond either person's insistence to support it. You could both have been wrong, and another attempt could have shown you where. That possibility belongs to the strength of the arrangement.

There is a world holding the pieces, the people and the consequences in the same encounter. It reaches us differently, and we describe it with unequal success, but it can resist a shared mistake as well as a solitary one. To understand how such comparison is possible, we have to look further than our confidence. We have to ask what the world is doing while we are busy explaining it.
