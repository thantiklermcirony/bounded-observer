# You Were Not There at the Beginning

You wake before the alarm and look through the window. Someone is already walking a dog. A delivery van is reversing with the solemn announcement that a delivery van is reversing. The tree outside has begun moving in a wind you cannot see. Your phone contains a message sent while you were asleep. The day has apparently started without obtaining your consent.

This is a serious administrative failure. You are the person through whom your entire experience of the universe must pass. Surely somebody could have waited.

Yet here it is: a world already underway, and you arriving in the middle of it with a face, a history and an opinion about the temperature. Even your objection depends on equipment you did not invent. The words arrive in a language that was spoken before you could speak. Your eyes open because a living body has made opening them possible. The window belongs to an arrangement of materials, work and ownership. You have entered a morning with an extraordinary amount already included.

Most of us begin our account of ourselves too late. We start with the person who can say “I”, then ask how that person meets the world. But the person is one of the things the world has been making. Before you had a description, you had a development. Before you could decide what mattered, things were happening on which your ability to decide would depend. The beginning of your account is not the beginning of your existence.

There is something intimate in that difference. A photograph of a newborn shows an individual already receiving a name. Outside the photograph are the meals, fears, bodies, rooms, journeys and relationships that made the moment possible. Some were chosen. Some happened to people who had little choice. An immense history has reached a place where someone can put a blanket around you and say that you have arrived.

You have. The announcement is important. It is also late.

Birth makes a new relation with the surroundings possible, but it does not start a person from nothing. In a study of prenatal sound exposure, newborns whose mothers had played a repeated speech-like sound during pregnancy showed stronger brain responses to certain changes in that sound. The finding concerns particular sound features and measured responses. Even before a child could recognize a word or give an account of hearing it, an encounter had left something available for a later encounter.

That is a strange thought to carry into the morning. Your first explanation of the world came from a being that the world had already affected. Later you would learn to tell the story. The telling can feel like an origin because it is the part you can possess in words. But language lights a room that was occupied before the light came on.

And the history goes farther back than a family can remember. Carbon and oxygen in our bodies were formed through the lives of earlier stars. Much of our hydrogen has an older origin in the early universe. The materials with which you are seeing this sentence have travelled through events that occurred before there was an Earth on which a sentence could be written. There is no need to turn this into a certificate announcing that you are cosmically special. The carbon in a cabbage has an impressive background too. It has simply chosen to remain quiet about it.

The wonder is not that prestigious material has ended up in your possession. It is that material can become part of a living arrangement through which a portion of the world is encountered. The same world that contains the tree contains the body seeing the tree. The encounter does not require a spectator to be installed outside nature, looking down through a celestial viewing hatch. Nature has produced participants who can notice, remember, ask and respond from somewhere within it.

This is where I begin the bounded observer. An observer belongs to what it observes. It has a route by which something can reach it, a way of registering what arrives, and a history that makes some arrivals useful or frightening or familiar. A human being is an extraordinary instance of this position. Being extraordinary does not remove the position.

Look again at the tree. You do not receive the whole of it. The roots are out of sight. You cannot see the water travelling through its tissues. The insects under the bark have not been consulted about the description you are forming. Perhaps you know the species. Perhaps you know only that it blocks the view. Both kinds of knowledge can enter the encounter, though they will prepare you for different questions.

Light reaches your eyes, where the retina converts it into signals that travel toward the brain. Seeing involves this bodily work, rather than a little tree being posted through the pupil for an inner person to inspect. Recognition arrives so smoothly that the work can disappear from experience. There is a tree. Of course there is. The extraordinary achievement of having a usable scene becomes the unremarkable background to deciding whether to close the curtain.

A limited view is how the encounter becomes available at all. You can see enough of the pavement to step onto it without needing an inventory of its minerals. A child can find a familiar face without first knowing the chemistry of skin. An organism's access is fitted to some differences, and those differences can guide what happens next. The world's richness exceeds any one such encounter. The encounter can still be accurate and useful.

There are animals for whom guidance requires much less than the scene you are enjoying. Researchers studying a marine worm larva found that illuminating a simple eyespot changed the beating of nearby swimming cilia. The eyespot could detect the direction of light without forming an image; the local connection helped steer the animal toward it. No tiny ocean had to appear inside the larva before it could move appropriately. Life had found a workable route from a difference in the surroundings to a difference in action.

Human experience includes far more. We can compare a present sight with a remembered place, wonder how the tree became crooked and imagine what the street would be without it. Still, the larva helps dislodge a habit of thought. Understanding an observer does not always mean finding the miniature theatre in which its version of the world is screened. Sometimes it means finding what can reach it, what the arrival changes, and what that change enables.

Time is already inside this problem. Experiments with bacteria have shown that their movement responses can depend on comparisons involving recent chemical stimulation. Altering the machinery of adaptation changes those comparisons. The present surroundings are encountered by a system that has retained something from earlier surroundings. That little carried difference can matter without becoming a memory someone tells at dinner.

Your own history is vastly more elaborate. A sound in the street may be ordinary to one person and alarming to another. The same words may offer reassurance or announce an obligation. It can be difficult to notice how much of the event arrives with you, because the result feels like one immediate thing. A message is rude. A room is welcoming. A person is trustworthy. The judgment can conceal the route by which a record, an expectation and a present condition met.

Consider the notification you found on the phone. It contains an ordinary request: could you do something today? Its length tells you very little about its size in a life. Someone unfamiliar with the sender might see a small favour. You might know the earlier promises, the unfinished task, the person who will be disappointed and the way a “quick question” has occasionally reproduced in captivity until it occupies an afternoon. The screen holds a few words. You supply the history that makes their practical meaning larger.

To call that meaning situated is to take it seriously enough to ask how it was made. It is not an invitation to declare every interpretation equally good. The request may really concern something urgent. You may have misunderstood it. The sender may have left out a fact. Another message, a conversation or the outcome of acting can change the account. Your first encounter belongs to the world and remains answerable to further encounters with it.

That answerability matters. It gives us a way to keep perspective without dissolving reality. The person who sees a branch across the path and the person who sees a shadow can disagree. Their disagreement is not settled by observing that each has a viewpoint. They can move closer, change the light, touch the object or ask someone with a clearer view. If a branch obstructs the path, an eloquent theory of shadows will eventually meet it with a shin.

We do not need to leave the universe to discover which account survives. We need a usable comparison within it.

Imagine that the object beyond your window seems motionless. A second person says it is moving. You discover that you have been attending to different parts: you to the trunk, the other person to the leaves. A disagreement about one object turns out to contain two different readings. Now imagine that you both mean the same leaf at the same moment and still disagree. You will need something else: a closer view, a record, perhaps a recognition that one of you is looking at the reflection in the glass. What looked like a contest between realities becomes a question specific enough for the world to answer.

This is a modest account of objectivity, and its modesty makes it powerful. No one receives a view from nowhere. People obtain observations from particular places, under particular conditions, and find ways to compare them. Instruments extend what can be distinguished. Records let a later person inspect an earlier encounter. Repetition can reveal whether an apparent relation persists. Another observer can point out what your method has systematically left outside the frame.

The achievement grows through those relations. You can know something you never witnessed because an account has travelled through routes that can be checked. You can learn from an instrument that senses something your body does not. The everyday world is full of such borrowed access. A weather warning joins measurements made elsewhere to a choice at your front door. A label on a product can carry knowledge of a process you have never seen. A friend's explanation can make an old remark intelligible in a new way.

Borrowed access also brings responsibility. A confident voice and a dependable route to information are different things. Sometimes a record is copied so often that its familiarity begins doing the work its evidence should have done. Sometimes a number has been measured carefully and then used to answer a question it was never meant to answer. The room may be full of people who agree because they have all encountered the same omission. Agreement is valuable when its history is understood. It can also be a queue in which everyone has mistaken the back of somebody else's head for a destination.

Notice how the scale has changed. We began with eyes at a window. We have reached the conditions through which a shared account can be made. There is no sudden moment when an individual leaves the physical world and enters a purely social one. The information travels through bodies, speech, cables, paper, buildings and institutions. Its meaning depends on what people can recognize and what they are allowed to do with it. A common world is maintained through these overlapping forms of access.

Some of the arrangement is so old that it can feel like the landscape. The street has a name. A name has a history. The language tells you which distinctions come easily into speech. The school helped teach what counts as a convincing answer. A workplace has decided what your attention is worth and when it should be available. By the time you can ask whether these are sensible arrangements, you have learned to ask partly through the arrangements themselves.

That is why “just think for yourself” is such a magnificently incomplete instruction. It is usually delivered in a language the speaker did not invent, through a device other people made, with a concept of independence acquired from a culture. The instruction may be worthwhile. It would be improved by acknowledging the staff involved.

Thinking for yourself can mean discovering which inherited account you are using, comparing it with something it has concealed, and taking responsibility for a judgment you can explain. It need not mean amputating every relation through which judgment became possible. A person who has learned from others is still a person. Independence can grow through the ability to examine an inheritance, rather than through the fantasy of having arrived without one.

There is an uncomfortable consequence. The world that made your starting position was not arranged with equal care for everyone. Some people receive time, safety and an audience for their questions. Others learn early that questions have costs. A person may recognize an injustice perfectly and lack the means to alter it. Someone else may be able to change a rule while having very little experience of what living under the rule requires. The position from which we observe includes the position from which we can act.

That inequality changes what a fuller account must contain. It needs the experience that a powerful observer has missed, and a route through which that experience can become consequential. Adding another point of view to a brochure is easy. Allowing it to change a decision can be much harder. A living account of the world has to follow the difference between being invited to speak and being able to affect what happens.

The bounded observer is therefore neither a solitary genius nor a helpless prisoner of a private scene. It is a participant with limited access, genuine capacities and relationships through which those capacities can change. This position gives inquiry its urgency. We need to discover which missing differences matter, how they can reach us, and what happens when we act on a better account. The limit supplies a question; the world supplies the consequences.

I find something deeply hopeful in this without needing to promise that every question has a happy answer. You do not have to become an unlimited mind to correct a mistake. You can learn why one path floods, why one task is difficult, why one number has been used badly. You can obtain enough understanding to change something specific. Sometimes the new information confirms a constraint. Knowing where a real constraint lies can spare a person the cruelty of being told that their difficulty is a failure to believe hard enough.

Our lives happen through both kinds of discovery: finding what the world permits, and finding which arrangements have made the permitted unavailable. A wheelchair user can know everything about a staircase and still need a ramp. Understanding helps identify the appropriate change. Materials, work, cooperation and a decision help make it. A better description can bring those requirements into view; the requirements still have to be met.

Return to the window. The tree has continued moving while we have been considering it. The dog has taken its human somewhere else. A vehicle passes carrying things somebody will need. Your morning contains a fragment of these lives and processes, along with the history through which you are interpreting them. You are seeing less than the whole. You are seeing something real.

The fact that the world formed you does not finish your story. It locates its beginning more honestly. You arrived into conditions, acquired ways of meeting them, and became capable of asking where those ways came from. Now your questions and actions enter the conditions another person will encounter. The world has made an observer who can participate in how the world continues.

That is a remarkable development for matter to have managed. It has built stars and cabbages, and somewhere along the way a being has appeared who can stand in a kitchen and complain that the universe has got the weather wrong. We can appreciate the absurdity and still take the being seriously. Behind the complaint are a body, a history and an expectation. Ahead of it may be a coat, a conversation, a changed plan.

But one condition has remained close enough to overlook. All this seeing and remembering is happening through a living body that must keep making it possible. It cannot be supplied once, like the window, and left to stand there. The body is doing something even while the observer feels still.

You turn from the glass. The morning is asking for your attention. First, though, this particular arrangement of the world needs breakfast.
