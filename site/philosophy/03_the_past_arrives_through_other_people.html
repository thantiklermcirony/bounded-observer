The Past Arrives Through Other People

Daniel John Murray · The World That Made You

Imagine finding an old wooden spoon at the back of a drawer. It belonged to someone who showed you how to make something you still cook. You have forgotten the exact afternoon. Perhaps the kitchen was smaller than it seems now. Perhaps the person was younger than you have allowed them to remain in memory. But your hand knows what to do with the spoon. You stir, taste, wait a little longer. A movement has survived its first teacher.

Then somebody beside you reaches for the salt, and a sentence comes out of your mouth. A familiar instruction, delivered in a familiar tone. You hear yourself saying it and briefly wonder who, exactly, is speaking. The words are yours. So is the breath. The expression appears to have arrived from a previous administration.

We usually notice inheritance when somebody leaves us an object. The object provides a convenient place to put the feeling. It can be held, polished, lost. Yet much of what reaches us has no place in a drawer. A way of speaking. A rule about when to apologise. The conviction that asking for help is sensible, or shameful, or something you should do only after producing an impressive amount of evidence that you have suffered enough.

These things can become ordinary before we learn that they were ever taught. By the time we begin to examine them, they may already feel like the furniture of the world. A child can dispute a rule. It is harder to dispute a rule that has been mistaken for the shape of reality.

You were named before you could answer. People interpreted your crying, decided what was likely to comfort you, made arrangements that you could not inspect. There may have been tenderness in those arrangements. There may have been neglect, fear or exhaustion. Other people's understanding was part of the environment through which your own understanding grew. Even resistance develops somewhere. The child who becomes determined never to resemble a parent has also been given something to organise a life around.

None of this removes the person who eventually appears. It tells us something about how a person becomes possible. The self is particular, with its own place in the world and its own experience. It also has a history of being fed information, being recognised, being misunderstood, being invited into activities and being kept outside them. A boundary can make someone distinct while allowing a great deal to pass through.

Consider the words you are using to think about this. You did not invent their basic meanings, negotiate their spelling or arrange their delivery to your childhood. A language was waiting, spoken by people who had received it from other people. You learned to enter its conversations. You could ask for a drink long before you could explain what a request was. Eventually you could describe something that none of those people had yet encountered.

That is one of the extraordinary things about an inheritance. It can carry the means of exceeding its previous use. A language that once taught you to obey can later help you formulate an objection. The words need not contain every possible thought in advance. People make new distinctions, coin terms, point, draw, demonstrate. We can struggle towards a meaning we do not yet know how to say. Having been formed by others does not confine us forever to repeating them.

The danger comes when a convenient description becomes a permanent allocation of possibility. Someone is called difficult, clever, unreliable, the practical one, the one who never quite gets it. Often there was an event behind the description. Perhaps several events. A child struggled with a task. A person broke a promise. A family needed someone dependable, so one member was appointed to the position and discovered that resignations were not being accepted.

Descriptions can help us respond. They also hide the conditions under which they were made. Difficult with whom? Unreliable about what? Unable to do the task after which instruction, with how much time, in what circumstances? A label travels more easily than the evidence that originally supported it. Eventually it arrives at a new situation wearing the uniform of an established fact.

The family constitution is particularly efficient at this. It can be issued verbally during a car journey and remain binding for forty years. There is rarely an appeals process. Somebody once lost a coat and is still introduced as the one who loses things. The coat has probably decomposed. The ruling is in excellent condition.

There are affectionate versions of these arrangements. There are versions that narrow a person's life. The difference matters. A shared joke that people can alter is different from a role enforced whenever someone tries to become anything else. We have to look at what happens when the description is questioned. Does the group learn something, or does it punish the person for making the old account inconvenient?

Access to language and records has never been equally available. In his account of his life, Frederick Douglass described being taught the alphabet by Sophia Auld, then having that instruction stopped by her husband. Douglass understood the objection to his learning as revealing something about the power exercised over him. He continued finding ways to learn, including lessons from boys he encountered in Baltimore. His account also describes the anguish of understanding his condition more clearly while still being held in it. Reading opened possibilities; it did not by itself abolish the institution that denied his freedom.

That history makes a comforting account of knowledge insufficient. Information can change what a person can see while leaving them exposed to the same violence. Someone may understand an arrangement perfectly and lack the power to escape it. To confuse understanding with freedom is to overlook the door, the guard, the wage, the threatened consequence. A language can make the demand expressible. The conditions under which it can be answered must also change.

Our less dramatic dependencies can be almost invisible because they work. You turn a tap and water comes out. You read a sign because other people have maintained a shared convention. You use a tool whose maker solved a problem you have never needed to solve. Someone explains a route, checks a figure, repairs a connection. A large part of human independence is supported by dependable arrangements outside the individual.

The self-made person, pursued with sufficient seriousness, soon becomes a rather strange construction project. They must grow the food, invent the words, make the road and establish a small legal system before arriving at the motivational conference to explain why everyone else should take more initiative. By then there may be nobody left in the audience. They are all busy manufacturing their own oxygen supply.

The joke does not make effort meaningless. Effort becomes more intelligible when we can see what it works with. A person's skill is real. So are the teaching, tools, opportunities and uncredited labour that help make the skill usable. Recognising those conditions lets us ask what might give somebody else a chance to develop it. It also lets us distinguish a contribution from a claim to have created every condition of its own existence.

Other people carry things we cannot keep alone. One knows the recipe. Another remembers why an arrangement was made. A third notices that the arrangement no longer does what it was supposed to do. A group can hold a larger useful record than any single member, provided there is a way to bring the relevant pieces together. This is an ordinary human possibility, with ordinary human difficulties. The person who remembers may be ignored. The person who decides may never ask.

We can be connected and remain strangers to important parts of each other's lives. Affection does not provide unrestricted access. Living in the same house does not make two histories identical. You can know somebody's expression and still misunderstand what has produced it. The other person has a body, encounters, private effort and things they have not found a way to explain. There is no scientific need to turn that relation into a mystical merger. Its incompleteness is already consequential.

Time deepens it. People who meet in one moment arrive through different sequences. The same request can follow a day of rest or a day spent being interrupted. A promise may follow years of reliability or years of delay. Even when the words match, the conditions that give them meaning can differ. What comes next depends partly on what has already happened and on what remains available to change.

A clock helps us meet. It does not tell us everything about the lives being brought to the meeting. Two people can be given the same afternoon on the same household clock, while one has quiet, transport and permission to use it, and the other is caring for someone with no replacement available. To call those afternoons equal opportunities is to ask the clock to certify a social arrangement it has never inspected.

Waiting also has conditions. A delay can allow something to settle, or use up the opportunity to respond. We know this in the kitchen: giving a mixture time is part of making it, and forgetting it on the heat is another action entirely. In human affairs, too, saying that time will sort things out can conceal the question of what is actually happening while everyone waits. Time passes through changing circumstances. It does not perform our repairs on our behalf.

We carry the past through memory, but memory is not a shelf on which every moment remains in its original packaging. Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed students films of traffic accidents and changed the wording of questions asked afterwards. Stronger collision wording affected speed estimates. In a later experiment, students first asked about cars that had “smashed” were more likely to report broken glass a week later than students asked about cars that had “hit”, although the film contained none. Their findings concern those reports in a particular experiment; they give us a concrete reason to examine how later language can enter an account of an earlier event.

This makes remembering worth taking seriously. A person can be sincere and still need to compare a recollection with another record. Conversely, the possibility of error does not entitle us to dismiss an inconvenient memory. We need the particular evidence. Treating every recollection as a perfect recording and treating every recollection as useless are both ways of avoiding the work of finding out.

The past arrives again when it is recalled, in the circumstances of the present. An old scene may now have a meaning its participants could not have given it then. That later understanding can be valuable. It should not quietly be inserted into their original experience. A retrospective account can see a pattern. It cannot automatically tell us what every person knew, intended or could have done at the time.

Forgetting belongs in this picture. Without some selectivity, a record becomes difficult to use. You do not need to remember every movement of the person who taught you to cook in order to make the dish. You may need to remember which ingredient went in first. You may need the warning about a substitution. If you are trying to preserve their life rather than prepare dinner, different details matter. What is enough depends on the question.

In the formal work behind The Bounded Observer, this is made precise for a declared class of records and future operations. Two histories can share one retained description when no allowed future question or action needs their difference. Change the questions and the description may have to change. This is a criterion for adequate retention, rather than an instruction that a human mind should reduce itself to a perfectly economical machine.

It gives the philosophy a practical task. Ask what a summary has lost before asking what its neatness has gained. A timetable can leave out whether someone has the transport to follow it. An attendance record can leave out the reason for an absence. A financial total can leave out when the money was actually available. The original facts may be accurately recorded while the conclusion drawn from their compression is wrong.

Australia's Robodebt scheme gives that last distinction a terrible concrete history. Annual income information was averaged and used in assessing supposed discrepancies in welfare payments. The Royal Commission reported that averaging without further evidence could not reliably establish entitlement, and described how people were treated as having income at times when they had not. It also documented legal and institutional failures. This was more than an awkward spreadsheet. Decisions were made with consequences for people who then faced the burden of challenging them.

Imagine two workers with the same annual earnings. One earns regularly; the other has long gaps and a short period of work. Their totals agree. Their circumstances during a particular fortnight may be very different. If that fortnight is what the decision concerns, the shared total cannot answer it alone. The missing distinction belongs to the decision. No amount of confidence in the arithmetic can put it back.

An institution can mistake the authority of its record for the adequacy of its knowledge. Once an entry has been accepted, each later office may treat the previous office's conclusion as an observation. A guess acquires a reference number. The reference number acquires a deadline. Eventually the person whose life contradicts it is invited to explain why they are causing a discrepancy.

Correction then requires more than a patient conversation between two perspectives. Somebody must be able to inspect the record, supply the missing information, challenge the inference and stop an unjustified consequence. Access to that process takes time and resources. Where the institution controls both the account and the available remedy, it matters who can demand a revision. Being bounded explains why an account can be incomplete. It does not excuse making it unanswerable.

This is why shared knowledge has to mean more than putting all the available sentences in one place. Records need origins and contexts. Some are direct observations; others are conclusions copied from elsewhere. Some people have been able to leave extensive records, while others appear only through an authority's description of them. A fuller history must ask whose opportunity to record was restricted, and what follows from those absences. An archive is a maintained human achievement, with human selection still inside it.

The world inside a person's account is therefore intimate and public at once. A parent's expression, a school rule, a work schedule, an official decision: each can arrive as something simply given. They come with different degrees of evidence and different powers of enforcement. The task is to learn how they have been made, which conditions they describe, and whether they remain appropriate to the life now before us.

That task can preserve affection. A person who taught you something does not have to have been right about everything for their care to matter. You can keep the recipe and reconsider the judgment. You can honour an effort without requiring the next generation to repeat the fear that accompanied it. Inheritance becomes more usable when it is possible to separate the gift from the demand that it never be altered.

Return to the spoon. It is still a useful object. The person who first held it is not secretly alive in the wood. Something of their work has reached this kitchen because objects were kept, movements learned, circumstances remembered. The life they lived remains different from the effects that continue. Those effects can meet a new person and take a form their first maker never saw.

Suppose a child beside you says they cannot do something, and you recognise the familiar sentence in your own reply: that is just how they are. For a moment it seems as settled as the recipe. Then you notice that the child is here, now, with a task that has conditions you could look at. You could change the instruction, give them time, ask what happened. The old description has arrived, but it has not done the investigation.

Your hand keeps stirring. You let the inherited sentence stop before it becomes the next person's inheritance. There is something you do not yet know, and somebody whose future may depend on whether you make room to find it out.
